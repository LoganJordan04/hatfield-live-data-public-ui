## Problem and Context

The Hatfield Live Data Public UI project involves making a website to display four of the Hatfield Marine Science Center's (HMSC) data streams in one place, making it easy and accessible for users to see all of the relevant information from the center's servers and websites. The four data streams were specified by our project partner and include: 1. Weather Station information from a CEOAS server, 2. Yaquina Water Quality from a data center VM, 3. Pumped Seawater SCADA from a local facilities server, and 4. OSU Underwater Dock Webcam [1]. The website is intended to be publicly accessible to everyone, as well as being displayed at one of the exhibits for visitors to explore at the HMSC. We've already implemented the layout for how the website would look for our first prototype. The home page of the site would display a map of the HMSC, with a button that enables users to access the data from each data stream. The location of the buttons correlates to where the actual dock instrument is used to collect the data on the map. Clicking on a button takes the user to the data page, where they can toggle between looking at the data and a learning page where they can learn more information about the data stream and answer some questions about it. One of the main goals of the project is to incorporate a K-12 educational component in some way so that the general public of ages 8+ will learn something from looking at our website. It's through the learning page where we hope to accomplish that goal. There are various ways to go about it, and we will need to ensure that the extra information we add to provide context for the data streams needs to be accurate. 

The decision we need to make is to decide on what sort of learning outcomes we want users to take away from after visiting our site, and how to go on about implementing features that enable users to meet those learning outcomes. This decision does not have to be decided asap, but it is a good idea to think about it now before it's too late. The educational component of the project could be added later on after we get the base requirements implemented (incorporating the four data streams on one website). Reaching a decision early on though will help drive our decisions for other aspects of the project and make it easier to develop the educational component/learning outcomes when we get to that stage, since we'd know exactly what to do by then. It is better to try to reach a decision sooner rather than later, especially this term when we have the most opportunity for planning requirements and specifications of the project out.

If the team did nothing at all regarding making a decision for this problem, that would not be good. Technically, the educational component of the project is a stretch goal, and just displaying the four data streams in one place would suffice as the definition of done, but the team and our project partner would really like to have this be incorporated. We feel that we'd have a lot of extra time to play around with the website and add extra features to it once we get the core requirements done, and developing a system that teaches users something would be quite fulfilling to us. Nothing bad would happen if we didn't add an educational component in, besides the users not really taking anything away after visiting the site. But having learning outcomes would help give the website a purpose and help retain user attention and engagement.

## Options

### Option A: Speak with project partner about educational component

This option involves speaking with our project partner, Marnie Zirbel, first and ask for her thoughts and opinions on how we should go about implementing the educational components of the website, and what learning outcomes she recommends. In a meeting with her, she expressed some thoughts on this already, and how she was planning on perhaps discussing the educational component with a K-12 educator board. Marnie is an expert in marine science, so she would provide very helpful insight for this aspect of the project, and guide us in the right direction. She would know what data to highlight and how to frame the data in a way to make it understandable and informative for people of ages 8+. Marnie has also lectured and taught audiences before, so her experience would be very valuable for this. She would know what information would be accurate or not, so she'd be a good fact checker and consultant. There's not really any major costs to this option, besides perhaps taking a bit of time to get all the information and recommendations we need from Marnie and/or a K-12 educator board.

### Option B: Add context and fun facts to the data displayed from the data streams

This option involves implementing the educational component and learning outcomes ourselves without consulting Marnie first, and then showing her our project as it progresses and once this part is done. The project is quite open ended, so Marnie would not likely have strong oppositions to this idea. As a team, we talked about how we could add contextual information to each data stream to describe the instrument that recorded the data and what the data means. We also thought it would be cool to include fun facts to each data stream to further educate users in an entertaining way. This option would likely be approved by Marnie and it has the benefit of informing users what each data stream represents, since without context, the data can be confusing - especially to someone without a marine science background. The costs would include the fact that we would do this option on our own first without consulting Marnie, being if she doesn't like how we did it, that'd be a good amount of progress down the drain. In addition, we would probably need to spend more time doing research and fact checking our information, since we do not want to include any context or fun facts that are inaccurate.

### Option C: Hybrid approach - Use information that our project partner gave us in shared drive and show her website prototypes

This option combines both Options A and B as a hybrid approach. Marnie has shared with us a Box folder with documents and information pertaining to the project, including documents that reveal what sort of learning outcomes she'd like for each data stream. She included context and sample questions for the data streams in these documents, which would be a good idea for us to reference as we build out the learning page for each data stream. We could use this information as a starting point to fill in the learning outcomes, and ask Marnie for clarifications along the way. We could show her figma mockups and website prototypes as we progress, to gather feedback and to see if she agrees with the way we implemented the learning outcomes. This option would work very well considering that we have the information from the Box folder, and gathering feedback from Marnie would be very insightful and help steer us in the right direction. There are no major costs associated with this option, besides potentially being not as effective compared to if we wanted to go all in with Option A or Option B, since this option is a jack of all trades.

### Option D: Do Nothing

Doing nothing and not implementing some sort of educational component/learning outcomes wouldn't be the end of the world, and the project could likely be defined as done without it, but it would make the project feel a bit lacking. Without this aspect of the website, I feel that there wouldn't be much purpose to the site. Sure, it would mean we complete the project faster, but since we have a full year to work on it, we might as well try to make it as complete as possible. Lacking this part would mean at the end of the year when our site is displayed online and at Hatfield, the users that go through our site wouldn't really take anything away from it. It could feel like they wasted their time, especially if they don't have a marine science background. Making a decision other than this one would mean that users will learn something, and feel happy and satisfed that they spent time out of their day to go through our site.

## Analysis

For Option A, it is the most surefire way to ensure that we are developing the project in a way that Marnie likes and agrees with. According to Leapwise, good engineering starts with communication, not code [2]. We're not exactly sure what she would recommend but we feel that it would be pretty similar to Option B. If the K-12 educator board gets involved, the learning outcomes could get pretty complex and involved. With an official structure though, maintainability would likely be simple. I'm not sure what structure that would entail though, as I am not on the K-12 educator board. The only cost of this option is that it could take a bit of time to sort everything out and cover all the specifics with Marnie.

For Option B, we could start development right away without needing to consult anyone, at least initially. The HMSC has a website for the Coastal Monitoring Station (CMS), which links to some of the data streams and could be a good reference for some of the context we'd put into them. The website would also help guide the questions we make and determine our learning outcomes. For example, the website states that the CMS measures salinity, temperature, depth, pH, dissolved oxygen, nitrate, etc [3]. We could display that information through the Yaquina Water Quality data stream, and ask questions about those results that would require users to carefully read the numbers and/or graphs. This option wouldn't be too complicated to add in addition to displaying the four data streams, since we'd just be adding relevant context to the data. This would also not be hard to maintain by keeping the information up to date. However, not running this option first through Marnie could result in big changes if Marnie did not like this direction we went in. We'd also need to fact check more on our own and it would be easier to let misinformation spread through the cracks, meaning users would see inaccurate information.

For Option C, this hybrid approach is a very efficient option. Working with Marnie and referencing the files in the Box folder, along with showing her our prototypes we develop along the way, helps reduce complexity with figuring out what sort of questions and learning outcomes we'd add. The files in the Box folder show us the relevant context and questions Marnie suggests we implement, and figuring things out together both as a team and with our project partner allows the benefits of both ways of communication to shine. One of the files in the Box folder is a pdf about the Yaquina Estuary, which we could use as context to supplement the learning outcomes that Marnie suggested. For example, the pdf says that an estuary is a place where freshwater and saltwater meet [4]. That information can be used as one of the answer choices to a sample question like "What is an estuary?" Consulting with Marnie and using these resources allows us to get information like that pdf, which otherwise we wouldn't have known about. Showing her our progress as we develop our website also invites her to provide feedback and refinements we could make, saving us time and helping her figure out what other directions the project could go in regarding learning outcomes. It's easier to work together than for collaboration to be strictly team vs project partner focused.

For Option D, doing nothing saves us the most time, but it would mean an important aspect of the project, in my opinion, would go unresolved and make the website feel a bit pointless. It is the least complex and it could save a bit on performance time, since there'd be less code that would need to be run. There would be nothing to maintain but our users would not really take anything away after visiting the website. People with a marine science background may know what the data streams mean with no context, but people without that background may feel lost and discouraged from visiting the website again. According to a Medium article, some consequences of not learning is intellectual stagnation, lack of adaptability, and loss of passion and purpose [5]. So not thinking about learning outcomes or an educational component while designing the website would not benefit anyone that intends to learn something from it. 

## Recommendation and Verification Plan

I propose that we go with Option C. It is the option with the fewest drawbacks and makes the most sense, as the hybrid approach combines both our ideas and our project partner's. Our project partner shared with us the Box folder so that we could reference the information there, so we would want to utilize at least some of those ideas for learning outcomes. Especially since she has teaching experience and a marine science background. The team will know this was the right choice through positive feedback from Marnie when we show her our prototypes, as well as users of our site successfully meeting the learning outcomes. We would show Marnie our progress routinely and confirm that we're on the right track in terms of the types of things she'd like people to learn from the website. In the testing stage and when we're gathering user feedback, we could assess how many questions users get correct for their chosen data stream page. We will likely quiz users in the style of multiple choice questions, and if they answer at least 75% of the questions correctly, then we will know that the decision worked and paid off. We could then personally ask the users what they think of our website and one thing they learned. If they can answer with something that was stated on our website, that also means our decision worked.

## Constraints

The project is not under any NDA and our project partner, Marnie Zirbel, has given us a lot of control and creative freedom over the project. The Box folder she shared with us contains information pertaining to possible learning outcomes, so we might have to use those sample questions as starter learning outcomes, but it's likely we will be able to build upon them too. We're not bound by many constraints which is nice.
